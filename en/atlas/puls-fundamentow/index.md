# The Fundamentals Pulse

We want to know whether the companies in the Nasdaq-100, taken together, are doing better than a year ago. We don't look at share prices for this, only at what the companies themselves report: how much they sold (revenue) and how much of it they kept (net income).

## Three views of the same picture

- **Latest quarter.** The freshest data. During earnings season it only includes the companies that have already reported.
- **Last 12 months.** Four quarters together, so a single weaker or stronger quarter doesn't change the picture.
- **Latest full fiscal year.** From the annual report. Not every company ends its year in December: some end it in January, others in June, so their years don't line up to the day.

We compare each of these numbers with the same period a year earlier.

## How we turn a hundred companies into one answer

- **The total for the whole index.** We add up the revenue of all companies, and separately their net income, and check whether the total is higher than a year ago. The biggest companies carry the most weight here, just as they do in the index itself.
- **The typical company.** We line up all the companies from the weakest result to the best and look at the one in the middle (that's the median). One exceptional jump won't move it.
- **Why not a simple average?** A single company with a one-off gain is enough to make the average jump by dozens of points and show something that isn't there across the index.
- **The Rising–Falling indicator.** A single number from 0 to 100 that shows what share of the companies is growing. It is the average of two percentages: companies whose revenue is rising and companies whose net income is rising. 0 means all are down, 50 half and half, 100 all are up.

Finally, one sentence: **rising** when both total revenue and total net income are up; **falling** when both are down; and when they move in different directions, the **results are mixed**.

## Where the numbers come from

From the reports the companies file with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC): quarterly reports, annual reports and earnings releases. When a company reports in, say, euros or yuan, we convert its figures to dollars at the Federal Reserve's average exchange rate for the same period. We always state how many companies the result covers.

## What this answer doesn't tell you

It's a snapshot of what has already happened, not a forecast. It doesn't say where the index will go, doesn't judge whether the results are good or bad for the market, and doesn't compare them with expectations.
